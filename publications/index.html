    Harty, J. S., & Rankin, L. Fathers carrying collective on tap: A community-based feasibility study of babywearing for fathers at local breweries. Perspectives in Infant Mental Health, 34(2).

    Fathers and fatherhood

    Journal PDF Details Google Scholar

    Fathers play a critical role in infant development, yet they remain systematically excluded from the medical, social service, and early intervention systems designed to support caregiving in the #rst year of life. Babywearing, the practice of carrying an infant in a cloth carrier worn on the body, is an evidence-informed caregiving practice studied almost exclusively with mothers. The Fathers Carrying Collective on Tap (FCCT) is a community-embedded babywearing intervention designed speci#cally for fathers and delivered at local breweries. This feasibility study examined whether fathers would enroll, engage, and sustain participation across two father- centered events in Arizona in Spring 2026. Feasibility indicators included recruitment yield, event attendance, weekly survey response rates, retention, engagement, and participant-reported satisfaction. Thirteen fathers enrolled at the Tucson event; the Phoenix event, launched without embedded community partnerships, yielded no attendees. Weekly follow-up response rates across the #rst four weeks were high (100%, 100%, 85%, 92%), and fathers reported approximately 58 minutes of daily babywearing on average, closely tracking the one-hour target. Satisfaction was uniformly positive. The contrast between sites indicates that established community partnerships, not the venue alone, drive recruitment. Findings support the intervention's feasibility and inform the design of a subsequent e!cacy-focused trial.

    Murray, B. J., Harty, J. S., & Dettlaff, A. J. Accountability or complicity? Social work’s dual commitments and crisis of accountability in the US. Critical and Radical Social Work, Preprint, 1–19.

    Social work history

    Journal Details Google Scholar

    The social work profession in the US espouses commitments to equity and justice while also being complicit in systems of racial and social oppression, revealing a persistent accountability crisis within the profession. This article traces the historical and contemporary dimensions of that crisis, asking: To whom is social work accountable? Through archival research and analysis of contemporary case studies, we trace how professional bodies have defined, regulated, and enforced accountability since the 1970s. We argue that professional regulation, codified during this period, aligned social work with state power by prioritizing risk management and professional legitimacy politics over accountability to marginalized communities. Amid authoritarian resurgence and the suppression of marginalized communities, these alignments demand renewed scrutiny. Using accountability as a central analytic, this article contends that ethical practice in social work requires moving beyond compliance with state regulation and toward collective responsibility to communities.

    Thomas, A., Walsh, T., Cryer-Coupet, Q. R., Harty, J. S., & Pate, D. The health implications of fatherhood: A comprehensive literature review. Annual Review of Public Health.

    Fathers and fatherhood

    Journal PDF Details Google Scholar

    There is growing recognition that paternal involvement matters for child development and family well-being, even while we know comparatively little about how fatherhood affects men&apos;s own physical and mental health. This review brings together our current understanding of how becoming and being a father shapes men&apos;s health and the current efforts at improving the fatherhood experiences for men, thus indirectly influencing their children and families. We explore emerging work on father support systems, from mobile health interventions to successful community-based programs. The review also considers how policy—both historic and current—has shaped fathers’ health and their capacity to be involved parents. Our analysis reveals substantial gaps, particularly around understanding the nuances of paternal support. We conclude by identifying where research efforts should focus next and what practitioners and policymakers need to consider, as attention to the impact of fatherhood on men&apos;s health continues to grow.

    Child welfare and systemsSocial work history

    Journal Details Google Scholar

    Despite the passage of the Indian Child Welfare Act nearly 50 years ago, Indigenous children are persistently disproportionately represented within the child welfare system, yet continue to be overlooked in the debate between abolition and reform in the social work literature. Purpose: As these disparities continue to widen, social workers fail to recognize the unique struggles of Indigenous children and families upon encountering the child welfare system. Design: The present work provides a historic overview of Indigenous child welfare interaction, grounds discussions in coloniality, and implicates social work’s complicity in perpetuating the genocidal fiscal economy of Native child trafficking. Results: Through a lens of Indigenous child welfare, we sift through the abolitionist versus reformist debate, introduce the Indian Child Welfare Act as an example of a non-reformist reform, and conclude with implications for social work practice.

    Harty, J. S., BlackDeer, A. A., Schiele, J. H., Francis, E. A., & Washington, D. M. Origins of opportunity, part 1: The formation of CSWE’s minority research fellowship program, 1954–1978. Journal of Social Work Education, 61(S1), S4-S20.

    Social work history

    Journal Details Google Scholar

    This paper explores the historical development of the Council on Social Work Education's (CSWE) Minority Fellowship Program (MFP), coinciding with the 50th anniversary of its research fellowship. By leveraging archival CSWE materials like meeting minutes, reports, letters, and newsletters, it chronicles key events from 1954 that culminated in the MFP's creation in 1973 and extends to the third research fellowship cohort in 1978, just before the introduction of the second clinical fellowship. The paper details the challenges and milestones that shaped early initiatives for diversity and inclusion within social work education. It offers a reflective examination of the MFP's inception, tracing the advocacy and structural changes within the CSWE and broader social movements that culminated in the creation of the MFP and its sustained impact on the profession. Established to address the underrepresentation of minorities in social work education, the MFP was designed to develop researchers and practitioners of color attuned and responsive to the varied needs of their communities. This paper not only enriches the social work historical record but also highlights the transformative impact of the MFP in cultivating a profession that truly reflects and effectively serves our racially and ethnically diverse society.

    Social work history

    Journal Details Google Scholar

    Over its 50-year history, the Minority Fellowship Program (MFP) has focused on increasing the number of practitioners and scholars trained to serve underrepresented and underserved racial and ethnic minority communities at risk of mental health and substance abuse issues. With over 1,000 individuals trained at the master’s and doctoral levels, the MFP has played a signicant role in preparing scholar-practitioners to make impactful contributions in addressing the needs of vulnerable populations. Through testimonios, we explore the career trajectory, collaborative partnerships, and community effect of a cluster of five doctoral-level MFP alums hired at a large public R1 university in the Southwest from 2016 to 2023. Our testimonios bear witness to our collective experiences as MFP alums and diverse faculty of color, particularly as they relate to our positionality before, during, and after our time as fellows. We examine the pivotal aspects of MFP participation, including its infuence on our engagement in antiracist, antioppressive, and decolonial initiatives and efforts to recruit and mentor graduate students of color. We close by sharing the enduring lessons and insights gained from the MFP and propose strategies for cultivating a diverse doctoral student pipeline in social work to address mental health and substance abuse issues.

    Park, S., Okpych, N. J., Harty, J. S., & Courtney, M. E. Co-Producing service experiences for transition-age youth in foster care: Exploring the connection between transitional independent living plan development and independent living service usage. Children and Youth Services Review, 178, 108550.

    Child welfare and systems

    Journal PDF Details Google Scholar

    In the U.S. foster care system caseworkers are charged with preparing older adolescents unlikely to be reunified with their families or be adopted for independent adulthood. An important tool is the transitional independent living plan (TILP), which involves collaboratively engaging transition-age youth in planning for their future, identifying goals, and determining service needs. The TILP guides which Independent Living Services (ILS) the youth may receive. Depending on the youth’s goals and needs, ILS may include training and support in education, career, parenting, and other areas. Past research on this topic has focused on the availability and effectiveness of ILS and TILP planning. Less is known about whether the frequency of TILP engagement is associated with ILS use. Using administrative data and representative longitudinal survey data, we examine the relationship between TILP development and ILS utilization patterns among older youth in California foster care. Our results show that youth developed less than one TILP per year, used about one ILS monthly, and used four different types of ILS while in care. Our regression results demonstrate that youth with more TILP engagement were expected to use more ILS and a broader range of ILS. Our results also highlight associations between the county’s administrative contexts and the youths’ ILS utilization patterns. Our study offers rare empirical evidence that underscores the importance of regularly co-producing future plans with youth, training caseworkers to work collaboratively with youth in decisions about their lives, and reducing between-county variation in TILP and ILS practices.

Using representative survey data of youth transitioning out of foster care in California, we examine the prevalence and predictors of food insecurity.     Over the past 30 years, there has been a surge of interest in understanding the experiences and outcomes of expectant and parenting foster youth. Despite the importance of understanding this unique population of foster youth, there remains a lack of research on fathers in foster care. Most studies of expectant and parenting foster youth focus on mothers in care, and studies that have examined fathers in care provide little insight compared to what we know about mothers.
